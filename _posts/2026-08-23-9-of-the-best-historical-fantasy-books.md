Most "best historical fantasy" round-ups sort books by century or by vibe: candlelight, corsets, a vague sense of Old Europe. Fewer of them ask what the magic is actually doing there. That's the more useful question, and it's the one historian and novelist P. Djèlí Clark gets at in his [Fivebooks interview on the genre](https://fivebooks.com/best-books/the-best-historical-fantasy-books-p-djeli-clark/): magic in historical fantasy tends to work best when it stands in for something real about the period rather than just dressing the set. Sometimes it's a stand-in for technology or industry. Sometimes it's what's left of a belief system the history books didn't have room for.

So that's how I've grouped the nine books below: not by century, but by what the magic is for. A few run on magic as raw infrastructure, the kind of power that only some people are allowed to touch. A few use it as a record of what a culture keeps when everything else gets paved over. And a few use it as an argument, a way of making a historical claim land harder than a footnote ever could.

One note before the list: I've kept this to books actually rooted in a real place and period, with the fantasy layered on top, rather than secondary worlds that just borrow historical texture. ## Magic As Power Structure

In this historical fantasy group, magic works like infrastructure rather than a talent. Who controls it, who's allowed to learn it, and who does the dangerous work underneath it says everything about how the society actually runs.

  <img src="https://covers.openlibrary.org/b/isbn/0063021420-L.jpg" alt="Babel by R.F. Kuang" style="width:120px; flex-shrink:0; border-radius:4px;">
  <div>
    <h3>1. Babel by R.F. Kuang (2022)</h3>
    <p>Kuang's magic runs on the gap between languages. Silver bars engraved with near-matching words in two tongues capture whatever meaning gets lost in translation, and that lost meaning is what powers everything from bridges to weapons. The catch is that it only works if the translator is genuinely fluent in both languages, which means 1828 Oxford has to import and train foreign students it has no intention of treating as equals.</p>
    <p>Robin Swift is one of them, brought from Canton to train at Babel, Oxford's Royal Institute of Translation, the tower the whole silver-working system runs through. For a while Oxford looks like a dream. Then it starts to look like a machine, and Robin has to work out what he's willing to do about it. The magic here isn't a metaphor bolted onto a colonial setting. It's the same extraction, running on the same fuel.</p>
    <p><strong>Connect with:</strong> Kuang's Poppy War trilogy, angrier and bloodier, if Babel's argument is what pulled you in.</p>

    <h3>2. Jonathan Strange & Mr Norrell by Susanna Clarke (2004)</h3>
    <p>It's 1806, England is deep in the Napoleonic Wars, and everyone believes magic has all but vanished from the country, until the reclusive Mr Norrell announces he can still do it. A second magician follows soon after: younger, wilder, far less interested in doing things the respectable way. Jonathan Strange becomes Norrell's pupil, and for a while the two of them work together to help win the war against France.</p>
    <p>What the book is really about is control. Norrell wants magic licensed, filed away, safely inside the establishment. Strange wants it usable, unpredictable, effective on an actual battlefield. Their falling out is a fight over who gets to decide what counts as legitimate power, and what gets left outside the door (the fairies, the old roads, the Raven King) because it refuses to fit the paperwork.</p>
    <p><strong>Connect with:</strong> Clarke's own <em>Piranesi</em>, a much shorter, much stranger novel with almost nothing else in common except the prose.</p>

    <h3>3. Temeraire (His Majesty's Dragon) by Naomi Novik (2006)</h3>
    <p>Same Napoleonic Wars as Jonathan Strange, entirely different kind of magic: dragons, intelligent and opinionated, flying alongside the navy on both sides. Captain Will Laurence captures a dragon egg from a French ship, and when it hatches he becomes bonded to Temeraire, effectively ending his naval career and starting a new one in Britain's Aerial Corps.</p>
    <p>What makes the series more than "Napoleonic Wars but with dragons" is that the dragons aren't weapons, they're people, with their own politics and loyalties. Dragons from China see themselves as part of Chinese civilisation. Dragons in later books, from parts of Africa touched by the slave trade, are furious about it and act accordingly. Novik uses the dragons to show how the same historical moment looks completely different depending on where you're standing.</p>

## Magic As Cultural Memory

A different category. In these books the magic works less like a system you could chart on a whiteboard and more like what's left over when a culture gets pushed underground. What the spell remembers usually matters more than the spell itself.

---

<div style="display:flex; gap:1.5rem; align-items:flex-start; margin-bottom:2rem;">
  <img src="https://covers.openlibrary.org/b/isbn/1101885955-L.jpg" alt="The Bear and the Nightingale by Katherine Arden" style="width:120px; flex-shrink:0; border-radius:4px;">
  <div>
    <h3>4. The Bear and the Nightingale by Katherine Arden (2017)</h3>
    <p>Set in Rus' before Peter the Great, in a country still caught between old and new. Vasya's household has always left offerings for the domovoi and the other spirits that keep a home and farm safe, a habit that goes back generations. Her new stepmother won't leave the offerings, partly because she's devout, partly because she genuinely can't see the spirits at all anymore.</p>
    <p>The magic here is quiet. It's a bowl of milk left by the stove, a habit that dies out one skipped winter at a time as the Orthodox Church tightens its hold on the village. Arden treats that slow erosion as the real horror story, and it's more unsettling than anything with teeth, because you can watch it happening and still not be able to stop it.</p>
    <p><strong>Connect with:</strong> <em>The Girl in the Tower</em> and <em>The Winter of the Witch</em>, the rest of the Winternight trilogy.</p>

    <h3>5. Spinning Silver by Naomi Novik (2018)</h3>
    <p>Novik again, this time working the Rumpelstiltskin story into something closer to an argument about debt and antisemitism in a fictionalised Eastern Europe. Miryem is the daughter of a moneylender too kind-hearted to actually collect what he's owed, so she takes over the family business herself and gets very good at it, which earns her a rumour that she can turn silver into gold. That rumour reaches the Staryk, the fairy king of a cold and merciless winter kingdom, and he decides to test it.</p>
    <p>The Staryk's ice magic runs on rules and bargains rather than spectacle, which makes it feel less like sorcery and more like a second economy sitting right alongside the human one, just as capable of ruining you if you don't read the terms carefully.</p>
    <p><strong>Connect with:</strong> Novik's <em>Uprooted</em>, a standalone in a similar register with a different fairy tale underneath it.</p>

    <h3>6. The Golem and the Jinni by Helene Wecker (2013)</h3>
    <p>Two immigrants arrive in New York in 1899. One is made of clay and built to obey a master who dies before the ship even docks. The other is made of fire, centuries old, and used to a freedom he can no longer fully use now that he's bound to a human shape. Neither belief system they carry with them survives the crossing intact.</p>
    <p>Wecker isn't really writing a fantasy adventure so much as an immigrant story that happens to have two people in it who aren't, technically, people, which turns out to be an honest way of writing about arriving somewhere new while still carrying the old country inside you. The golem's magic is obedience built into her nature. The jinni's is a freedom he keeps reaching for and can't quite get back.</p>
    <p><strong>Connect with:</strong> the sequel, <em>The Hidden Palace</em>, which picks the story back up a decade on.</p>

## Magic As Historical Argument

The hardest category to pull off. Here the fantasy element works less like a system and more like a thesis, a way of making a historical claim more legible than a footnote could manage on its own.

---

<div style="display:flex; gap:1.5rem; align-items:flex-start; margin-bottom:2rem;">
  <img src="https://covers.openlibrary.org/b/isbn/9781250267689-L.jpg" alt="A Master of Djinn by P. Djèlí Clark" style="width:120px; flex-shrink:0; border-radius:4px;">
  <div>
    <h3>7. A Master of Djinn by P. Djèlí Clark (2021)</h3>
    <p>Clark's alternate Cairo, 1912: djinn are real, walking around with jobs (some are lawyers, apparently), and magic has turned Egypt into a serious world power almost overnight. Agent Fatma works for the Ministry that exists specifically to manage all of this, and she's pulled into a case involving a man claiming to be al-Jahiz, the figure who tore open the veil between worlds decades earlier.</p>
    <p>What makes this land as more than a fun steampunk mystery is the history underneath it. In our world, European powers carved up Africa at the Berlin Conference. In Clark's Cairo, they're desperately trying to work out how to contain a country that magic has made too powerful to carve up. It's a clean, clever way of putting colonial anxiety on the page without lecturing about it.</p>
    <p><strong>Connect with:</strong> <em>The Haunting of Tram Car 015</em> and <em>A Dead Djinn in Cairo</em>, shorter stories set in the same Ministry.</p>

  <img src="https://covers.openlibrary.org/b/isbn/9781250767028-L.jpg" alt="Ring Shout by P. Djèlí Clark" style="width:120px; flex-shrink:0; border-radius:4px;">
  <div>
    <h3>8. Ring Shout by P. Djèlí Clark (2020)</h3>
    <p>Clark again, and a very different register. It's 1922 in Macon, Georgia. Seven years earlier, the film <em>The Birth of a Nation</em> reignited the Klan across America, and in Clark's telling it did something worse than that: it opened a door, and some of the Klansmen riding now aren't entirely human anymore. Maryse Boudreaux and her fellow monster hunters are the ones trying to close that door before it opens all the way.</p>
    <p>This is the book on this list that makes its argument least subtly, and it isn't trying to be subtle. Turning the Klan's ideology into something literally monstrous is the whole point: racism here summons the demon directly, no metaphor required. It's a short, brutal, propulsive read that doesn't let you look away from what it's actually about.</p>
    <p><strong>Connect with:</strong> <em>The Black God's Drums</em>, Clark's debut novella, set in an alternate post-Civil War New Orleans.</p>

  <img src="https://covers.openlibrary.org/b/isbn/0316377910-L.jpg" alt="The Monsters We Defy by Leslye Penelope" style="width:120px; flex-shrink:0; border-radius:4px;">
  <div>
    <h3>9. The Monsters We Defy by Leslye Penelope (2022)</h3>
    <p>Washington D.C., the Roaring Twenties. Clara is trying to live a fairly ordinary life among scholars and neighbours in a Black community that also happens to have crime bosses with their own magical enforcers on the payroll. A cursed ring pulls her into all of it, alongside a crew she has to assemble to help her get free of it again.</p>
    <p>The title comes from a line in Claude McKay's 1919 poem "If We Must Die," written in response to the lynchings and anti-Black riots of that year's Red Summer, and Penelope builds a lot of the book around real details of the era that tend to get flattened in the popular memory of it, drag balls for queer and cross-dressing Black men included. The folk magic running through the book feels like part of the same historical record as the poem it borrows its title from, not decoration laid on top of it.</p>
    <p><strong>Connect with:</strong> Penelope's <em>Song of Wrath</em> if you want to see her working the same instincts into a full secondary-world epic instead.</p>

## Frequently Asked Questions

### What's the difference between historical fantasy and historical fiction?

Historical fiction stays inside what actually happened or could plausibly have happened. Historical fantasy borrows the setting and texture of a real period, then adds magic, gods, or invented history on top of it. Where the line gets blurry is books like Diana Gabaldon's Outlander, which is otherwise grounded, real 18th century Scotland with one time-travelling exception, and tends to get shelved as historical fiction rather than fantasy because of it. If you want the real thing without the magic, our guides to [Kate Quinn](https://www.litloop.co/blog/kate-quinn-books-in-order/), [Conn Iggulden](https://www.litloop.co/blog/conn-iggulden-books-in-order/), and [the best WW2 historical fiction](https://www.litloop.co/blog/best-ww2-historical-fiction-books/) are good places to start.

### Do I need to know the history first to enjoy these?

No. Every book on this list works as a story on its own terms. But knowing roughly what actually happened, the Opium Wars behind Babel, the Klan's resurgence behind Ring Shout, tends to sharpen what the author is doing with the magic rather than being required reading beforehand.

### Which one should I start with?

If you want the clearest version of the "magic as empire" argument, start with Babel. If you want something quieter and more folkloric, The Bear and the Nightingale. If you want the shortest, hardest gut-punch on the list, Ring Shout will do it in under 200 pages.

## Final Words

The thread running through all nine of these is restraint, oddly enough, the same thing that makes good historical fiction work. None of these authors throw magic at a historical setting just to make it more exciting. The magic is doing a job: propping up a system, holding onto what a culture is losing, or making an argument that history itself sometimes struggles to state plainly. Once you start reading for that, it's hard to read historical fantasy any other way.
