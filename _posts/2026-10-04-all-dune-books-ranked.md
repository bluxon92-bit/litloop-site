*Dune* is a book I put off reading. I'd heard about it for years, and every time I picked it up I looked at the size of it and put it back down.

It also turns out to be more than one book. Frank Herbert wrote six Dune novels, and after his death his son Brian Herbert and co-author Kevin J. Anderson added seventeen more. That makes 23 novels written over almost sixty years.

Eventually I plucked up the courage and started, and Herbert did not disappoint. If you want something big, with politics, interstellar travel, giant sandworms and prophecies, *Dune* is a good place to start.

This post ranks all 23 novels by their Goodreads rating, and adds my own notes on Herbert's original six.

## Frank Herbert's original six

Here are the original novels in order of Goodreads rating, with my notes on each.

### Dune (1965) ★ 4.29

Paul Atreides arrives on Arrakis, the desert planet that is the only source of the most valuable substance in the universe, and everything changes. Herbert builds a world with a lot of detail. Ecology, politics, religion, economics and power are linked closely enough that a change to one affects the rest.

The detail always serves the story and the ideas Herbert is working through. It is also the only book in the series with more than a million Goodreads ratings.

### Children of Dune (1976) ★ 3.95

Paul's children, Leto and Ghanima, deal with a world changed by the events of the first two books. The political plotting deepens, the ecological and religious themes develop, and the consequences of *Dune* are still playing out. It has the highest rating of Herbert's five sequels.

To me this feels like the last book that stays true to the first novel's mix of politics and ecology.

### Dune Messiah (1969) ★ 3.90

*Dune Messiah* picks up after Paul has taken power. Where *Dune* follows his rise, *Messiah* looks at what it costs him. Herbert has said he wanted to show the dangers of following charismatic leaders, and this is the book where that idea plays out.

It is shorter and darker than *Dune*, and it reads differently from the first book, so it helps to go in knowing that. It is also the book Denis Villeneuve is adapting for *Dune: Part Three* (more on that below).

### God Emperor of Dune (1981) ★ 3.88

Set 3,500 years after *Children of Dune*, this is the biggest jump in time the series has made so far. Leto II has become a hybrid of human and sandworm, and he rules the known universe with a plan that spans thousands of years. The book is philosophical, light on action and slow in places.

I lean towards thinking it is Herbert's most ambitious book, though I won't pretend the first hundred pages aren't hard going.

### Chapterhouse: Dune (1985) ★ 3.85

Herbert's last Dune novel, published the year before he died. The Bene Gesserit face an existential threat, and the story builds towards a cliffhanger he never got to resolve. The ending is incomplete, but it is still recognisably Herbert and still strange, and it is worth reading if you have come this far.

### Heretics of Dune (1984) ★ 3.84

Another 1,500 years on. The empire has fractured and new power structures are forming from the wreckage. *Heretics* puts the Bene Gesserit at the centre and deals with gender, sexuality and power more directly than the earlier books. It reads to me as a return to form after *God Emperor*.

## Reading Dune Messiah before Dune: Part Three

*Dune: Part Three* is due in cinemas on 18 December 2026. It is the final film in Villeneuve's trilogy and it adapts *Dune Messiah*. Villeneuve has said the film will stay fairly faithful to the novel.

If you want to read the book first, *Messiah* is shorter than *Dune*, so there is time to finish it before the film arrives. If you would rather go in without knowing the story, it works just as well after the film. Either way, read *Dune* first, because *Messiah* starts where that book ends.

## Brian Herbert and Kevin J. Anderson novels

After Frank Herbert died, his son Brian Herbert and co-author Kevin J. Anderson wrote seventeen more Dune novels. They are by different authors from the original six, and on Goodreads their ratings run from 3.71 to 4.03. That overlaps with the range for Herbert's own sequels (3.84 to 3.95), and it puts the top-rated continuation, *The Butlerian Jihad*, above all of them.

Keep the sample sizes in mind, though. Most of these books have far fewer ratings than the original six, so a small gap between two of them says very little.

### Picking up where Chapterhouse stopped

Working from Frank Herbert's notes, Brian Herbert and Kevin J. Anderson wrote two books to finish the story. If you want closure after *Chapterhouse*, these are the ones that provide it. The rest of the continuation novels are optional.

## Dune books in order

There are two ways to order the books, and they give different results.

**Publication order.** This is the order Frank Herbert's readers got them in, and it is the one I'd use for a first read:

1. Dune (1965)
2. Dune Messiah (1969)
3. Children of Dune (1976)
4. God Emperor of Dune (1981)
5. Heretics of Dune (1984)
6. Chapterhouse: Dune (1985)
7. Hunters of Dune (2006), if you want the story continued
8. Sandworms of Dune (2007), to finish that story

The prequels and interquels can come after that, in any order.

**Chronological order.** This follows the story's timeline, which starts with the prequels:

1. Legends of Dune (three books)
2. Schools of Dune (three books)
3. Prelude to Dune (three books)
4. The Caladan Trilogy (three books)
5. Princess of Dune
6. Dune
7. Paul of Dune
8. Dune Messiah
9. The Winds of Dune
10. Children of Dune
11. God Emperor of Dune
12. Heretics of Dune
13. Chapterhouse: Dune
14. Hunters of Dune
15. Sandworms of Dune

For a first read I'd go with publication order. Chronological order puts the prequels in front of the book that introduces the world. I'd go into *Dune* knowing as little as possible, because finding things out alongside Paul is part of the experience.

## Where to start

**Start with *Dune*.** Read it before anything else, and then see how you feel.

**Read the original six in order if you want the full story.** That includes *God Emperor*, even if the pace tests your patience.

**Leave the prequels until after.** They are optional, and they read better once you know the world they are set in.

## Dune books FAQ

**How many Dune books are there?**

This guide covers 23 novels: six by Frank Herbert and seventeen by Brian Herbert and Kevin J. Anderson. Other lists reach higher or lower totals depending on whether they count short stories, collections and comics.

**What order should I read the Dune books in?**

For a first read, publication order: *Dune*, *Dune Messiah*, *Children of Dune*, *God Emperor of Dune*, *Heretics of Dune* and *Chapterhouse: Dune*. The full list, including chronological order, is in the reading order section above.

**Which Dune book has the highest Goodreads rating?**

*Dune*, at 4.29 from more than 1.7 million ratings. The highest-rated book after that is *The Butlerian Jihad* at 4.03, and the highest-rated of Frank Herbert's sequels is *Children of Dune* at 3.95.

**Do I need to read all the Dune books?**

No. The original six make up the main story. *Hunters of Dune* and *Sandworms of Dune* continue it from *Chapterhouse*, and the rest are optional.

**Are the Brian Herbert and Kevin J. Anderson books worth reading?**

It depends on what you want from them. Their Goodreads ratings range from 3.71 to 4.03 so they stand by their own merit. And if you are looking for more from the Dune world, and you've devoured the rest of the books then they are the natural next step.

*Hunters of Dune* and *Sandworms of Dune* are the two that finish the story Frank Herbert left open, and the prequels and interquels are extras.

**Should I read Dune Messiah before Dune: Part Three?**

You don't have to, but the film adapts that book. *Dune Messiah* is shorter than *Dune*, so it is possible to read it before the film's release on 18 December 2026. Read *Dune* first.
