*The Knight and the Moth* has been all over [BookTok](https://www.tiktok.com/) and Bookstagram since it came out in May 2025, and it is easy to see why. The cover is gorgeous, the premise is a prophetess and a rude knight, and the whole thing is wrapped in cathedral mist.

I wanted to know whether the book lives up to the cover. Short answer: mostly, with one caveat. It is a gothic romantasy first and a dark fantasy second, and your enjoyment depends a lot on which of those you came for.

**Short on time?** Read it if you want atmosphere, a slow-burn rivalry and a late twist. Skip it, or borrow it first, if you want a fantasy that stays dark all the way through.

## What is The Knight and the Moth about?

Sybill Delling, who [everyone](https://) calls Six, is one of six diviners living at Aisling Cathedral in the kingdom of Traum. She can't remember much of her life before the cathedral. What she does have is almost ten years of drowning and dreaming, which is how the diviners learn the fates of the pilgrims who travel there. Her service is nearly over, and the six of them have started to wonder what comes next.

Then the new young king visits and brings a knight with him. Roderick Myndacious is brash, irreverent and openly unimpressed by the diviners and their dreams. Sybill hopes he leaves quickly. Instead her fellow diviners begin to vanish one by one, and she runs out of people to turn to. That leaves her with the king and his very difficult knight, and a quest to find out who is behind it.

That is as far as I'll go. The set-up is the hook, and the book is better if you meet the rest cold.

---

## What works

**The opening.** The cathedral and its drowning pool give the first act real gothic weight. Aisling is built from familiar faux-medieval parts, but the diviners' ritual gives it a strange, specific shape. Most readers who loved the book point to this section first.

**The gargoyle.** A talking gargoyle who calls everyone Bartholomew sounds like a bad idea. It works. He supplies the comic relief, his mixed metaphors are a treat, and he is one of the reasons to keep reading to find out whether he is more than a stone sidekick.

**The religion and the magic.** The magic is dark and has a price, and the religious lore feeds into the plot instead of sitting in the background. Booklist praised the worldbuilding and plot, and Publishers Weekly called the premise fresh. Both seem fair to me.

**The last quarter.** The late twists are the best argument for finishing. You may spot some of what's coming, but the book also takes a sharp turn away from the ending you'd expect, and it makes book two an easy sell.

## What might not work for you

**The romance is a big share of the story, and it is a familiar one.** Sybill takes an instant dislike to a tall, brooding knight. If you know the enemies-to-lovers map, you'll see the route early. If you came for the slow burn, that's a feature. If you wanted romance as a subplot, it isn't.

**The tone shifts.** Once Sybill leaves the cathedral, the gothic dread loosens and the book reads more like a traditional fantasy quest. Plenty of readers won't mind, and quests are a staple for a reason. But if the opening sold you a gothic dark fantasy, the middle may feel like a change of genre.

**It isn't grimdark.** I'll say this plainly for anyone who comes to Litloop for the darker end of the shelf. The book has dark magic and a bleak premise, but its heart is romantic and it's pacing is brisk. ## How it compares to Rachel Gillig's earlier books

Gillig's first series is the Shepherd King duology, *One Dark Window* and *Two Twisted Crowns*, which is also romantic, atmospheric fantasy. Some readers who bounced off those found this one better, and one reviewer thought the characters here were more nuanced. Others, including a reader who found *One Dark Window* only okay, finished this one feeling much the same.

If you liked the Shepherd King books, expect more of what you enjoyed. If they left you cold, this one is a gamble worth a library copy.

- Slow-burn, bickering-to-tender romance
- Quests with a strong supporting cast, including a stone one

## What do other readers think?

Readers have been warm. At the time of writing, the book sits at about 4.2 on Goodreads from more than 250,000 ratings. Ratings drift over time, so treat that number as a snapshot.

Critics have been more mixed than readers. The more reserved reviews tend to land on the same two points: the predictable romance and the loosening of the gothic mood. Fans, who are many, tend to love exactly those things.

## My verdict

I read this after labouring through a 1,000 page literary tome (review of that is incoming whilst I digest), so this was a real change of pace. Easy, enjoyable, a littleforgettable if I'm being honest. But, if you enjoy a romantasy that spends a lot of time on its central pairing, it's a good read. If you need more darkness, you may find yourself wanting the cathedral back.

## The Knight and the Moth FAQ

**Is The Knight and the Moth a standalone?**

 No. It's the first book in the Stonewater Kingdom, which is billed as a duology.

**Do I need to read Rachel Gillig's other books first?**

No. The Shepherd King duology is a separate series, and this one is a fresh start.

**Is The Knight and the Moth dark fantasy or romantasy?**

Both, but it leans romantasy. The opening is gothic and dark, and the romance is a major thread throughout. For more of the genre, see our [best romantasy books of all time](https://www.litloop.co/blog/11-best-romantasy-books-of-all-time-ranked/).
