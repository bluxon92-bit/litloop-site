No historical period has produced more historical fiction than the Second World War, and for obvious reasons. It's the last conflict within living memory for anyone still alive to tell you about it directly, and it left behind an almost unmanageable wealth of real stories: resistance networks, codebreakers, survivors, home fronts held together by people who had no choice but to hold them.

I've already covered a few WW2 essentials in my broader [best historical fiction books](https://www.litloop.co/blog/best-historical-fiction-books/) list. This guide goes deeper, organized by the different angles the genre tends to take on the war. If you want to browse further afield once you're done here, the [historical fiction genre hub](https://www.litloop.co/genres/historical-fiction/) has the wider list.

## Holocaust And Survival

---

<div style="display:flex; gap:1.5rem; align-items:flex-start; margin-bottom:2rem;">
  <img src="https://m.media-amazon.com/images/I/71hq9YctuHL._SL1250_.jpg" alt="The Tattooist of Auschwitz by Heather Morris" style="width:120px; flex-shrink:0; border-radius:4px;">
  <div>
    <h3>5. The Tattooist Of Auschwitz by Heather Morris</h3>
    <p>Based on the real testimony of Lale Sokolov, who was forced to tattoo identification numbers onto fellow prisoners at Auschwitz-Birkenau, and who fell in love with one of them. Morris has written it more as a novel drawn from years of interviews with Sokolov than as strict history, which has drawn some criticism over factual accuracy, but the emotional core is undeniable.</p>

Sepetys specializes in exactly this: the corners of WW2 history that don't usually make it into fiction.</p>

The naivety of the narration is the whole point, and it's exactly what makes the ending land as hard as it does. It's a shorter, simpler read than most of this list, and often a reader's first introduction to the Holocaust in fiction.</p>

## Prisoners Of War

---

<div style="display:flex; gap:1.5rem; align-items:flex-start; margin-bottom:2rem;">
  <img src="https://m.media-amazon.com/images/I/71Eu5wjdRxL._SL1500_.jpg" alt="King Rat by James Clavell" style="width:120px; flex-shrink:0; border-radius:4px;">
  <div>
    <h3>9. King Rat by James Clavell</h3>
    <p>Set inside Changi, the Japanese POW camp in Singapore where Clavell himself was held during the war, following an American corporal who's built himself a small empire running the camp's black market while everyone around him starves. It's a much colder, harder book than most of this list, more interested in what captivity does to hierarchy and morality than in heroism. If you want your WW2 fiction to sit somewhere other than Europe, this is the one to start with.</p>

  <div>
    <h3>17. Suite Française by Irène Némirovsky</h3>
    <p>Unlike everything else on this list, this one wasn't written with hindsight. Némirovsky drafted it while living under German occupation in France, before her arrest and death at Auschwitz in 1942. The manuscript survived in a notebook kept by her daughter for decades before it was finally read and published in 2004. It follows French civilians fleeing Paris and then living alongside occupying German soldiers, and the immediacy of it, written by someone who didn't know how the story would end, is what sets it apart from every other book on this list.</p>

## Final Thoughts

What separates the best WW2 historical fiction from the merely competent is specificity: the real convoy, the real codebreaking room, the real ship most people have never heard sank. Every book on this list is doing that in a different corner of the war, from the resistance networks and the concentration camps to POW camps, the home front, and the quiet acts of survival that don't usually make it into the history books at all.
